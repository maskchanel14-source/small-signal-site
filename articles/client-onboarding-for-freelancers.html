Every freelance project starts with a week you weren't paid for

Get the kickoff packet templates

Kickoff is where a solo business quietly loses its margin. Not scope creep — that's later, and by then you're already tired. Kickoff: the gap between "we have a deal" and "I know exactly what happens next", filled with chasing, guessing and re-explaining.

Here is the uncomfortable part. Most freelancers handle kickoff by being smart and fast. That works for two clients. It fails at five, and it fails completely the month you get the flu.

Below is the five-step version we use, with the three things you can copy out of it tonight.

Why week one is expensive

Look at what actually happens after a client says yes:

  • They email assets at 11pm on a Friday, in a zip, half of them duplicates.
  • You ask for access. They ask which access, and why. Two more emails.
  • You start work before the brief is finished because you don't want to look idle.
  • You agree to a 20-minute "quick call" to align. It is 55 minutes and it changes nothing.
  • Three days in, they ask for a status update. You write one from memory.

Total cost: five to nine hours of unbilled time on a project you priced at 30. Worse, the shape of the relationship is now set — they learned that you reply instantly, chase nothing, and start before you're ready.

Onboarding is not politeness. It is where the terms of the whole engagement get enforced, while the client still cares.

The five steps

1. Deposit before work. Not after the proposal, before the calendar invite. If your agreement says 50% up front and you still start on a promise, you don't have a deposit policy, you have a suggestion.

2. One kickoff packet, sent by you. Not a form they forget, not a call. A single message with five blocks: what we're building, what's out of scope, what I need from you (with dates), how we'll communicate, and what happens in week one. Every client gets the same packet, so writing it once pays forever.

3. Assets and access in one basket. One shared folder, one naming rule, one deadline. "Send me your stuff" is how duplicates happen.

4. A comms channel with a rule. Email or Slack or Notion comments — pick one, write down why, and say out loud that DMs to personal accounts don't count as instructions. That single sentence is worth more than most contracts.

5. A status update they never have to ask for. Weekly, same day, five bullets, sent even when there's nothing to report. Silence is what generates the "just checking in" email that costs you 20 minutes of context.

That's the whole system. There's no software in it.

The kickoff call, if the client insists on one

A kickoff call is not onboarding. It's a 50-minute conversation where two people discover they had different projects in mind - and nothing written down.

If you take the call, take it after the packet lands, and make it short on purpose:

  • Send the kickoff message first, and say when you send it: *"Read this before the call. Anything

you disagree with is cheaper to fix now than on the call."*

  • Cap it at 25 minutes, with the three open questions from your own read as the agenda.
  • End with a spoken summary, then write it into an email within 10 minutes: *"What we agreed:

X by Friday, you're sending Y, the risky part is Z. Reply 'correct' or correct me."*

That last line does something no call can: it turns a conversation into a record. When scope drifts in week three, you're not arguing from memory - you're pointing at an email they replied "correct" to.

Copy 1: the kickoff message

Send it the day the deposit lands, before any work starts.

Subject: [Project] - kickoff, and what I need from you by Friday

Hi [name] - excited to get going. Two things before I start:

1) Deposit received, so I've blocked [dates] on my calendar for this.
2) Here's everything I need from you to keep it moving.

WHAT WE'RE BUILDING
[one paragraph: outcome, not tasks. e.g. "A pricing page that raises the average
ticket, shipped as a Figma file plus a coded section in Webflow."]

OUT OF SCOPE (so we don't discover it in week three)
- Copywriting beyond the headlines we agreed
- More than 2 revision rounds per section
- Third-party subscriptions or hosting costs

WHAT I NEED FROM YOU
- Brand assets + any existing copy: one shared folder, by [date]
- Access: [tool], invite to [email]
- One decision-maker for approvals: [name]

HOW WE COMMUNICATE
- Weekly update from me, every [Tuesday], five bullets
- Questions: email or [Slack channel]. Messages to my personal accounts
  don't count as instructions - they get lost, and I won't bill for rework caused by that.
- Ad-hoc calls only when there's an agenda. I'll send 3 bullet points first.

WEEK ONE
- Day 1-2: I review assets, come back with any blockers
- Day 3: [first milestone, e.g. wireframe of the 3 sections]
- Day 5: you approve or send consolidated feedback in one pass

If any of the above is wrong, tell me today - it's cheap to change now.

[you]

Read it back. It does four jobs at once: sets expectations, documents scope, names the approver, and pre-prices the behaviour you don't want.

Copy 2: the asset request that stops the zip-file chaos

Folder: [client] - [project] - assets
Structure (please use these exact names):
  /1-brand    logo (svg+png), fonts, brand pdf
  /2-content  existing copy, docs, screenshots
  /3-access   logins or invites (via [1Password/Shared link], not email)
Anything missing by [date] = the schedule moves by the same number of days.
I'd rather chase once, politely, than restart twice.

That last sentence is the one that feels rude the first time you send it. It is the reason you stop feeling rude later.

Copy 3: the weekly update format

[Project] - week 3 update

DONE       Hero section copy approved, shipped to staging
NEXT       Two supporting sections, review by Friday
BLOCKED    Nothing
DECISION   Do we keep the "compare plans" toggle? (yes/no is fine)
HOURS      11.5 of 20 budgeted

If I don't hear back by [date], I'll proceed with the safer option and note it here.

Five lines. Written in four minutes with a prompt if you want one: "Here are my notes from this week: [paste]. Write a five-bullet client update in DONE/NEXT/BLOCKED/DECISION/HOURS format, 90 words max, no adjectives."

The "if I don't hear back" clause is the actual product. It converts silence from a delay into an approval, which is the difference between a project that slips and one that ships.

The out-of-scope list, by trade

Blank proposals get argued with. Specific exclusions get accepted, because they read like experience rather than fear. Steal the ones that match what you sell:

Design - copy you didn't write, print versions, source file handoff beyond the agreed formats, more than two revision rounds per section, animations added after sign-off.

Development - content entry, third-party subscription costs, hosting setup on accounts you don't control, browser support outside the agreed list, any change to a spec you already had approved.

Copy and content - fact-checking claims you weren't given sources for, SEO beyond on-page, publishing or scheduling, additional formats (the newsletter is not the blog post).

Video - music licensing, footage you didn't shoot, subtitle translation, more than two cut rounds, export sizes beyond the delivery list.

Marketing - ad spend, lead quality, anything that happens after the click, reporting periods shorter than a month.

Bookkeeping and finance - catching up months you weren't engaged for, tax advice, chasing client invoices on their behalf, payroll.

Two rules make the list work. One: every exclusion has a price next to it, so it's an upsell and not a refusal ("Print version: about 2 hours, say the word."). Two: read it back before you send it and ask what you've been burned by in the last six months. If a real wound isn't on the list, add it.

Three numbers to write down per project

Not revenue. These three, in a sheet, one line per week, thirty seconds:

  1. Hours logged vs hours budgeted. The gap is your real rate. If a $4k project ate 60 hours, you

bill $66 - and next time you'll price differently because you have the number, not the feeling.

  1. Days from "yes" to assets in hand. This is the kickoff metric. Over 7 days on average means your

packet isn't being read, and the problem is upstream of you.

  1. Revision rounds used vs allowed. Three rounds on a two-round project is the clearest signal that

either the brief was thin or the approval process is broken - and those need different fixes.

Most solo businesses know their revenue and none of these three. Revenue tells you how the year went. These tell you how the next project should be priced.

When the client ignores step two

It happens on maybe one project in ten: they never send assets, they answer the kickoff with "just start something".

Do not be heroic. Send this once:

Happy to start. Two options, your call:
A) I begin with placeholder content, you replace it later - I'll bill the swap as
   a separate small task (about 2 hours).
B) We shift the schedule by the number of days the assets are late.
Either is fine. I just need one of them in writing before I open the file.

You're not being difficult — you're being legible. Clients forgive a moved date. They do not forgive a rushed project that arrived anyway and missed the point.

Offboarding starts in the kickoff message

The proof you'll need in six months - a quote, a number, a logo in a case study - gets decided in the first week, not the last. Two sentences in the kickoff, and the ask stops being awkward:

At the end I'll ask for two things: a short quote about what changed for you, and permission to
show the work. If either is a no, say so now and I'll never ask.

One more thing worth stealing: while the project is fresh, write the result down in one line in your own notes - the number, not the adjectives. "Rebuild, 3 weeks, +18% demo requests." You will not reconstruct that from memory in month four, and a case study without a number is just pictures.

The 45-minute version

If you take nothing else from this, do this tonight, in order:

  1. Turn the kickoff message into a snippet in your email client or text expander. 20 minutes.
  2. Create the four-folder asset structure once, blank, and store it as a template. 10 minutes.
  3. Write the one sentence about where instructions count. 5 minutes.
  4. Set a recurring weekly reminder named "update, even if there's nothing to say". 5 minutes.

Four small things, and the next five projects start at a different speed.

None of this is AI work. AI is how you keep it cheap — drafting the update, classifying a change request, turning a call transcript into the "what we agreed" list. The structure has to exist first. AI is a very fast intern who has never met your client.
